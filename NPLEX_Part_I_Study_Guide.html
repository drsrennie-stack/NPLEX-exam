Exam Mechanics

Format, Scoring, and What You're Being Tested On

Structure of the exam

  • 200 multiple-choice questions, one best answer of four options
  • 50 case clusters, four questions per cluster
  • Two sessions on the same day, 100 questions each, 2.5 hours per session
  • Clinical summary is brief; the diagnosis is given to you
  • Questions probe the biomedical basis for the patient's condition, not treatment

The two General Exam Areas

  • Structure/Function (60%): anatomy, physiology, biochemistry & genetics
  • Disease/Dysfunction (40%): microbiology & immunology, pathology
  • ~40 questions per Specific Exam Area
  • You must pass both GEAs independently; failing one fails the whole exam
  • Cut score set by the Angoff method, not a fixed percentage

Highest-yield categories

  • Cardiovascular and GI tie for largest weight at 12% each
  • MSK, neurological, reproductive, urinary all at 10%
  • Endocrine, immunological, pulmonary at 8%
  • Heme and integumentary at 6%
  • Pathology and anatomy historically the lowest-pass-rate sections

Strategy fundamentals

  • Always mark an answer. Blank is scored as wrong.
  • Eliminate two distractors, then guess from remaining two
  • No trick questions. If it seems easy, it probably is.
  • You typically need 60-70% correct to pass
  • Pace: 90 seconds per question average, less on early ones to bank time

Universal Clinical Reasoning Patterns for Part I

Part I reasoning lens
Part I is not asking you to choose the right treatment or set the right diagnosis. The diagnosis is given. Your job is to explain the biomedical mechanism that links the diagnosis to one specific finding, structure, or pathway in the question. Read every stem with this lens: "They told me the disease. They're asking me which step in the mechanism explains the finding they're pointing at." This converts vague clinical pattern recognition into focused recall of one anatomical, physiological, biochemical, microbiological, or pathological fact.
Reasoning templateThe four-step Part I question read
1. Locate the diagnosis in the stem (always given). 2. Identify which SEA the question targets (anatomy, physio, biochem, micro/immuno, or pathology). The answer choices give it away faster than the question. 3. Run the mechanism chain from diagnosis to finding. 4. Eliminate distractors by checking which other diseases they belong to. Three of four options are usually correct facts for a different disease.
System 01

Cardiovascular System

Blueprint weight 12% Hypertension, CHF, IHD, valvular, congenital Tied for largest exam section

The cardiovascular system shares the top weight with GI at 12% of the exam. High-yield areas: cardiac cycle and conduction, lipid transport and atherosclerosis, congenital lesions and their embryological origins, valvular pathology with murmur patterns, and the hemodynamic categories (shock, embolism, edema). Expect heavy overlap with pulmonary, renal, and endocrine cases.

Coronary territory mapping
LAD occlusion = anterior MI (leads V1-V4). RCA occlusion = inferior MI (II, III, aVF). Circumflex occlusion = lateral MI (I, aVL, V5-V6). Posterior MI shows reciprocal ST DEPRESSION in V1-V2 with tall R waves.

Edema: the four Starling forces
Edema results from imbalance of hydrostatic pressure (pushing out of capillary), oncotic pressure (pulling back in), vascular permeability, or lymphatic drainage. Increased hydrostatic = CHF, venous obstruction. Decreased oncotic = nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, malnutrition. Increased permeability = inflammation, sepsis. Blocked lymph = filariasis, post-mastectomy.

Part I reasoningWhen the case gives you a coronary artery, give them the territory
NPLEX cardiovascular case clusters often anchor on a single coronary artery. You can predict the next 3 questions: (1) which artery supplies what region, (2) which lipoprotein/enzyme is implicated, (3) which embryological structure the affected chamber arose from, and (4) which complication comes at which post-MI time point. The diagnosis is rarely the question; the mechanism chain underneath it is.

Part I reasoningRead the lab pattern, not just the diagnosis
Endocrine NPLEX questions love to give you HIGH or LOW values for a hormone, its trophic hormone, AND its end product, and ask you to localize the lesion. The trophic hormone tells you primary vs secondary: low end-hormone + high trophic = primary gland failure. Low end-hormone + low trophic = pituitary or hypothalamic problem. High end-hormone + low trophic = autonomous gland (tumor or Graves). High end-hormone + high trophic = ectopic production (small-cell lung CA making ACTH) or resistance.

Immunology is conceptual: cytokines, HLA, complement, hypersensitivity classification, antibody isotypes. Few rote facts, many mechanism chains. The four hypersensitivity types are the single most testable framework. Expect cross-over into every other system because autoimmune diseases live everywhere.

HIV mechanism in one paragraph
HIV is a retrovirus that binds CD4 + a co-receptor (CCR5 on macrophages, CXCR4 on T cells). Reverse transcriptase converts RNA to DNA, integrase inserts it into the host genome. Patients with CCR5 delta-32 homozygous mutation are resistant to HIV. Progressive CD4 T-cell depletion leads to inability to mount cell-mediated immunity, so opportunistic intracellular pathogens predominate.

Part I reasoningMap the infection pattern to the immune deficit
Immunodeficiency clusters give you a recurrent infection PATTERN and ask which immune component is broken. Recurrent encapsulated bacteria = humoral defect (B cells, complement, spleen). Recurrent viruses, fungi, intracellular bacteria = cellular defect (T cells). Recurrent catalase-positive organisms = neutrophil defect (CGD). Recurrent Neisseria = terminal complement (C5-9). Recurrent giardia, sinopulmonary infections = IgA deficiency.

Part I reasoningLesion morphology IS the question
Skin questions are pattern-matching exercises. Lock in the basic descriptor vocabulary: macule (flat, <1cm), papule (raised, <1cm), plaque (raised, >1cm), vesicle (fluid-filled, <1cm), bulla (fluid-filled, >1cm), pustule (pus), wheal (transient raised pruritic), nodule (deep, palpable). When the diagnosis is given, the question is which microscopic layer is affected or what antibody is at fault. Pemphigus vs pemphigoid: "vulgaris is inside (intraepidermal), pemphigoid is below (subepidermal)."

Musculoskeletal System

Blueprint weight 10% Arthritides, neoplasms, infectious Heavy anatomy: muscles, bones, joints

CLINICAL DECISION-MAKING
Working through reproductive case clusters
Three high-yield Part I frameworks: (1) Amenorrhea workup always starts with hCG. After ruling out pregnancy, the FSH/LH ratio and estrogen tell you the level of failure: high FSH = ovarian (Turner, premature ovarian insufficiency, menopause); low FSH = hypothalamic/pituitary; normal-ish with hyperandrogenism = PCOS. (2) Testicular vs ovarian tumor mapping: germ cell origin is the most common in both, but the markers are the same (AFP from yolk sac, hCG from choriocarcinoma, both negative in seminoma/dysgerminoma). (3) STD discharge triage: pH and wet mount answer almost everything. pH less than 4.5 with pseudohyphae = candida. pH greater than 4.5 with clue cells = BV. pH greater than 4.5 with motile organisms = trichomonas. Painful vs painless ulcer separates HSV/chancroid from syphilis.

Urinary System

Blueprint weight 10% Glomerular, tubular, infectious Acid-base and RAAS

Urinary at 10% is the integrator system: acid-base balance, fluid status, blood pressure regulation, and electrolyte homeostasis all live here. The nephritic-versus-nephrotic split is the single most testable framework. Expect renal physiology questions that require you to trace Starling forces through Bowman capsule, follow Na/Cl/K/H/HCO3 through each tubule segment, and integrate RAAS with ADH and aldosterone. "COLA" cystinuria transporter
Hereditary defect in PCT transport of Cystine, Ornithine, Lysine, Arginine. Only cystine is poorly soluble → stones. Hexagonal crystals, recurrent stones from childhood.

Stones by composition + urine pH
Acidic urine (pH <5.5): URIC ACID and CYSTINE (radiolucent). Alkaline urine (pH >7): CALCIUM PHOSPHATE and STRUVITE (radiopaque). Calcium oxalate: any pH, most common, envelope crystals, radiopaque.

Pre/intrinsic/post-renal AKI in one line
Pre-renal: kidney works fine but starved (BUN/Cr >20, low FENa). Intrinsic: kidney is the problem (active sediment). Post-renal: blocked downstream (hydronephrosis on imaging).

CLINICAL DECISION-MAKING
Reading the urinalysis like a story
For Part I, urinalysis findings are the most efficient way to localize disease. Casts tell you the segment: RBC casts = glomerulus, WBC casts = tubulointerstitium (pyelo or AIN), muddy brown casts = ATN, fatty casts = nephrotic, waxy casts = end-stage. Crystals tell you the stone: envelope/dumbbell = oxalate, coffin lid = struvite, rhomboid = uric acid, hexagonal = cystine. Protein patterns tell you the lesion: selective albuminuria = minimal change disease (charge barrier loss), nonselective = FSGS or membranous (size barrier loss), Bence-Jones = multiple myeloma. When the kidney question gives you a creatinine bump, your first move is always to classify pre-renal vs intrinsic vs post-renal using BUN/Cr ratio, FENa, and the sediment.
